The Grupetto Committee meets on the last Tuesday of every month in the back room of a bakery, a quorum being any three members and a bicycle, and it was founded on the principle that somebody ought to write down who finished first, ideally somebody who never did. Its members, in the order they usually arrive:

## Ambrose Bidon, Chair

**Ambrose Bidon** took up cycling at the age of four, when a misunderstanding at a village fête saw him entered in, and narrowly lose, the 1903 Tour de France. He has been trying to make up the time ever since.

His palmarès is long and almost entirely unverifiable. He rode the whole of Paris–Roubaix on a penny-farthing and finished, by his own account, "somewhere near Roubaix, in a field, next to a cow who had clearly been sitting on the wheel all day." He descended the Stelvio backwards in order to enjoy the view, and holds the unofficial hour record for distance covered while holding a croissant in each hand.

Ambrose was briefly the leader of the Vuelta a España after a wrong turn outside Burgos put him three days ahead of the peloton. Race officials later ruled that being lost is not the same as being in front, a decision he still describes as "harsh, but fair, but mostly harsh."

His training methods are unorthodox. He rode the Mortirolo carrying his grandmother's upright piano; the piano placed third in the mountains classification and was offered a professional contract. In the spring classics of 1998 he drafted behind a flock of migrating geese from Ghent to Wevelgem and was relegated for an irregular sprint. The geese were not.

These days Ambrose rides a bicycle with a basket, mostly to the bakery, at a pace he describes as "aggressively tempo." He was elected Chair while lost during the vote, and builds this site in the evenings.

*The bicycle with the basket is real. Nothing else above is.*

## Marguerite "Peg" Lanterne, Treasurer

**Marguerite Lanterne**, Peg to the peloton, is the most decorated last-place finisher in the history of the sport. She has been lanterne rouge in thirty-one Grand Tours, four of which she did not enter, and the organisers eventually let her keep the lantern. She reads the accounts by it.

Peg's tactical instincts are legendary and entirely inverted. She was dropped on a descent. She attacked in a neutral zone, was neutralised, and attacked the neutralisation. Her longest solo breakaway began in the start village and ran the wrong way for nine hours, ending in the previous day's finish town, where she was warmly congratulated by people who had waited around.

In 1996 she was handed the green jersey by a confused official. She wore it as a scarf until 2003 and gave it back with a note that said "lovely, but not mine."

Her palmarès does include one outright victory: the 1988 Tour of the Low Countries, which she won by being the only rider to notice that the finish line had been painted on the wrong bridge. The others are still, technically, racing.

She rides today at what she calls "the pace of a considered opinion," usually to the sea and back, and is Treasurer because she is always the last to leave.

*The sea is real. The thirty-one lanterns are not.*

## Professor Cornelius Sprocket, Head of Science

**Professor Cornelius Sprocket**, DPhil (Aerodynamics, unaccredited), invented the tailwind in 1961 and has been in a dispute with the weather about the patent ever since. His wind tunnel is a garden shed with a leaf blower in it, and his findings have been published in every journal that has a letters page.

He is the father of marginal gains, having discovered them while weighing his own eyebrows. He then proved, with a blackboard and a small dog, that a rider can draft behind their own reputation, and that this saves roughly four watts per podium. Every equation on the blackboard ends in "= coffee".

His own hour record was disallowed because, in the commissaires' words, "the hour went by too quickly." He appealed on the grounds that this was precisely the point. The appeal is ongoing, as is the hour.

The Professor's greatest contribution to the sport is the Sprocket Principle: any rider who is behind is merely waiting for a more aerodynamic moment. He has applied it to thirty years of club time trials and to a lifetime of arriving late for dinner.

He now rides a heavily instrumented bicycle whose sensors, he reports, "mostly measure the sensors," and regards this site as a control group.

*The shed is real. The tailwind was there before he was.*

## Old Tom Chainwhip, Head of Cobbles

**Old Tom Chainwhip** rode the 1954 Tour of Flanders in wooden clogs on a bicycle he had made from a gate. He finished fourth, and would have won, he says, had the gate not still been attached to the fence.

Tom regards the cobbles of the Koppenberg as "gravel that has given up." He won Paris–Roubaix in the year they forgot to hold it, riding the full course alone on a Sunday because nobody had told him, and presented himself at the velodrome to an empty stand and one man sweeping. The man sweeping gave him the cobble. Tom keeps it in the kitchen and uses it to hold the door open.

His training is simple. He rides into a headwind until the headwind apologises. He famously said nothing at all for the whole of the 1961 season, and when asked afterwards how it had gone, replied, "It was fine." He still shaves his legs with the edge of a cobblestone and has never once explained why.

Tom does not believe in gears, cafés, or the month of July. He has not spoken at a committee meeting since 2009, which the minutes record as "agreement."

*The gate is real. It is still on the fence.*

## Isadora "Izzy" Échappée, Roving Correspondent

**Isadora Échappée** has been in every breakaway since 1994 and has never once been caught, on the technicality that she is still out there. Her contributions to this site arrive from the road.

Izzy won the mountains jersey on a course with no mountains by bringing a small hill with her. She lapped the field in a race that had no laps, and remains the only rider ever awarded the combativity prize in a time trial, for what the jury described as "a very determined attitude towards the clock." She sprints only in the mornings and considers the afternoon "more of a rolling situation."

Her time gaps are measured in weather. She was once four seasons ahead of the peloton and finished the Giro in time for the following one, which she also attacked from the start.

She lives in a caravan shaped like a bidon that follows the calendar from the Tour Down Under to Il Lombardia, and reports results with the authority of someone who was, in every sense, up the road when they happened. She has never attended a meeting and is considered the Committee's most reliable member.

*The caravan is real. It is not shaped like a bidon, and it does not move.*
